# Siting and Planting: Finding the Perfect Home

The most important decision you will make for your Japanese Maple happens before you even start digging. "Right Tree, Right Place" is the golden rule. A maple planted in the right spot will thrive for decades with very little help from you. A tree in the wrong spot will struggle no matter how much you care for it.

This chapter helps you find that perfect spot and shows you how to plant your tree correctly from day one.

![Siting and Planting Guide]({{ '/assets/images/siting-planting.jpg' | relative_url }})

---

## 1. Sunlight: The Morning Friend and Afternoon Foe

While maples need sun to grow and show off their colors, too much hot sun can burn their delicate leaves.
*   **Morning Sun is Good**: Try to find a spot that gets direct sun in the morning. This is cool light that helps the tree grow without getting too hot.
*   **Afternoon Shade is Better**: The afternoon sun (from 1:00 PM to 4:00 PM) is the harshest. If possible, pick a spot where the tree is in the shadow of the house or a fence during those hot hours.
*   **The Green and Red Rule**: Generally, green-leafed maples need more shade than red-leafed ones. If your red maple turns green in summer, it might need a bit more sun!

---

## 2. Wind: Protect Those Delicate Leaves

Japanese Maples have very thin leaves that can dry out quickly in the wind.
*   **The Problem**: Strong wind strips the moisture away from the leaves faster than the roots can replace it. This leads to brown, "shredded" leaf edges.
*   **The Solution**: Don't plant your maple in a wide-open, windy corner. Instead, look for a spot near a fence, a wall, or other larger trees that can act as a windbreak.

---

## 3. The Species Selection Factor

Your choice of species dictates how much protection you need to provide.

*   ***Acer palmatum***: These are the "generalists." While they prefer protection, many upright green and red cultivars can handle a full day of sun if they are well-watered.
*   ***Acer japonicum* & *shirasawanum***: These "Big Leaf" species are significantly more sensitive. Because their leaves are so large, they act like solar panels and wind sails. They **must** be sited in deep, filtered shade or a northern exposure, and they require a "dead-calm" pocket of the garden to prevent their broad leaves from tattering.

---

## 4. Drainage: No "Wet Feet"

More Japanese Maples are killed by too much water than by too little. They hate having their roots sitting in a puddle of water.
*   **The Test**: Dig a small hole and fill it with water. If the water is still there an hour later, your soil drains too slowly.
*   **The Mound Fix**: If your ground is heavy clay and holds water, don't dig a deep hole. Instead, place the tree on top of the ground and build a "mound" of soil up around it. This ensures the main roots are always above the waterline.

---

## 5. Soil Requirements: What Maples Want Underground

Sun and wind get the attention, but soil decides whether a maple merely survives or truly thrives. In the wild, Japanese Maples grow on forested mountainsides: loose, humus-rich, slightly acidic ground that never stays soggy. Recreate that, and the tree largely cares for itself.

*   **Texture: loose loam, not clay, not beach sand.** Squeeze a handful of moist soil: good maple ground forms a loose ball that crumbles when poked. Rock-hard clods mean heavy clay (suffocates roots — see [Chapter 14]({{ site.baseurl }}/chapters/14-soil-root-health.html)); soil that won't hold any shape means pure sand (dries out faster than roots can drink).
*   **pH: slightly acidic, 5.5–6.5.** This is the range where maples absorb nutrients properly. Test with a cheap probe or kit from any garden centre — guesswork here causes yellow, chlorotic leaves that look like disease but are just locked-up nutrients. Too alkaline (above ~7.0)? Work in small doses of elemental sulfur and retest in a few weeks. Too acidic (below ~5.0)? A light application of garden lime raises it.
*   **Organic matter: generous, but on top, not just in the hole.** Compost-rich ground holds moisture between waterings and feeds soil life. But don't dig a small hole and fill it with compost while the surrounding ground stays poor clay — that creates a "bathtub" that traps water around the roots. Instead, blend compost and bark fines broadly through the whole planting area, and plan to top-dress with compost yearly.
*   **Heavy clay?** Plant high using the mound method in Section 4, and consider gypsum on the surrounding clay to gradually open its structure (see [Chapter 14]({{ site.baseurl }}/chapters/14-soil-root-health.html)). Choosing a vigorous cultivar also helps — delicate variegated types suffer first in poor ground.
*   **Pure sand?** Blend compost through the planting zone and commit to a permanent 2–3 inch bark mulch ring — sand needs the organic sponge far more than loam does.
*   **Go easy on fertilizer at planting time.** Maples are light feeders, and raw fertilizer in the hole can burn new roots. Let the tree settle, then feed lightly in spring per [Chapter 7]({{ site.baseurl }}/chapters/07-fertilization.html).
*   **Finish with mulch, not a volcano.** A 2–3 inch ring of pine bark out to the drip line conserves moisture and cools roots. Keep it pulled back a few inches from the trunk — mulch piled against bark rots it.

> [!TIP]
> If you remember one soil rule, make it this: roots need air as much as water. Every soil decision — mound in clay, compost in sand, mulch everywhere — serves that single principle.

---

## 6. Amending by Soil Type: Fix What You Have

First, identify your soil with the **jar test**: half-fill a straight-sided jar with soil, top up with water, shake hard, and let it settle for 24 hours. Sand sinks in seconds, silt in minutes, clay stays cloudy for hours — the layers reveal your proportions. Sandy soil feels gritty, clay feels sticky and molds like pottery, silt feels smooth and soapy. Then amend as follows:

*   **Heavy clay (sticky, slow-draining):** the most common maple-killer. Never dig a deep hole and backfill with compost — that "bathtub" drowns roots. Instead, fork compost and bark fines broadly through the top 8–12 inches over the *whole* planting area, plant high on a mound (Section 4), and spread gypsum on the surrounding clay to gradually open its structure. Never add sand to clay — the mixture sets like brick. Mulch permanently.
*   **Sandy soil (gritty, drains instantly):** the opposite problem — water and nutrients wash straight through. Blend generous compost (and composted manure if available) through the planting zone to build a moisture-holding sponge, mulch 3 inches deep, and water more often but still deeply. Mulch matters more here than anywhere else.
*   **Silty soil (smooth, crusts over):** fertile but compacts easily and seals the surface. Work in compost and bark fines for structure, never dig or walk on it when wet (that destroys its pores), and keep it mulched so rain doesn't bake a crust.
*   **Good loam (crumbly, drains in minutes):** you won the lottery — do the least. A yearly compost top-dress plus bark mulch is all maples ask for. Resist "improving" it with extra amendments.
*   **Chalky or alkaline soil (pH above ~7.0, white flecks or fragments):** the hardest case, since maples need acidity. Apply elemental sulfur in small doses and retest over weeks, mulch exclusively with pine bark or pine needles (both acidify as they decay), and never add lime or wood ash. If pH refuses to budge, grow in a large container with the mixes in [Chapter 8]({{ site.baseurl }}/chapters/08-containers.html) instead of fighting the ground.
*   **Compacted ground (new builds, paths, lawn):** construction soil is often compressed subsoil with no life in it. Break up a wide area with a fork (not just the hole), work compost through it, and plant on a slight mound. Roots spread sideways, so a wide loosened zone beats a deep narrow hole every time.

> [!TIP]
> Amend the *area*, not the *hole*. Roots venture far beyond the planting pit within a few years — a broadly improved zone with native soil in the hole grows a stronger tree than a pocket of perfect mix surrounded by hostile ground.

---

## 7. How to Plant: The "Donut" Technique

When you are ready to plant, follow these simple steps:
1.  **Don't Dig Too Deep**: The hole should be wide, but keep it shallow. You want the "flare" (where the trunk becomes roots) to be visible above the ground.
2.  **Use Native Soil**: Don't fill the hole with fancy potting soil. Using the same dirt that's already in your garden forces the roots to grow out and get strong.
3.  **Water Deeply**: Once the tree is in the ground, give it a long, slow soak to settle the soil.

---

## Summary: Think Like a Tree

Before you plant, spend a day watching the sun and wind in your garden. Look for a spot that feels cool, protected, and has good drainage. If you build a solid foundation today, your Japanese Maple will reward you with a lifetime of effortless, spectacular beauty.
